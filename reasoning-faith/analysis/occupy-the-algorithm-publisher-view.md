# Occupy the Algorithm: A Publisher's-Perspective Evaluation

**Prepared for:** Drew (author, *Reasoning Faith*)
**Subject:** *Occupy the Algorithm* by Evan DiDio (working title: *YouTube for Churches*) — Christian nonfiction teaching churches to evangelize through YouTube and digital media
**Basis:** The full working manuscript (ten chapters plus reference material), which also preserves two rounds of editorial audit notes and the author's revision layers — an unusually candid look at how the book was built. Public information on the published edition is thin; DiDio is media director of Encounter Today (Encounter Ministries, North Carolina) and president of DiDio Media. This evaluation treats the manuscript as the primary evidence.

---

## 1. Positioning: Who It's For, What It Solves, and Why the Title Change Was Right

### Audience

The book knows exactly who is reading it, and it is not a general Christian audience. It is written to two overlapping people: the **volunteer or staff church media director** (DiDio's own role — he writes "Media Director (You)" at the top of his org chart) and the **pastor of a small-to-mid-size church** who suspects the church should be doing more online but has no time, budget, or team. Every chapter answers that specific person's next objection in order: *we don't have time* (Chapter 3's 30-minutes-a-day schedule), *we don't have money* (a shoestring gear list built around a smartphone and a lapel mic), *our people are too old for YouTube* (his data point that 28.2% of his channel's views came from viewers 65+), *is a secular platform even appropriate?* (the Lester Sumrall television argument). The book's audience discipline is one of its strongest acquisitional assets: an editor can name the buyer in one sentence.

### Felt need

The need the book sells is not "learn YouTube." It is a spiritual indictment dressed as a practical problem: your sermon dies on Sunday afternoon, and the camera at the back of your sanctuary — which DiDio says "represents the lost masses" — is being wasted. Chapter 1 stacks the pressure deliberately: an opening story of a witch wandering into a Wednesday-night livestream, statistics on churchgoers vetting a church's channel before ever visiting, and then the Sumrall passage, which reframes *not* using your smartphone for the Gospel as a failure Sumrall himself would punch you for. The felt need is guilt plus opportunity, and it lands because it is aimed at people who already believe in the message and merely need permission and a system.

### Comparable titles

On a shelf, this book sits between two categories and borrows the best of each:

- **Church communication books** (Nona Jones's *From Social Media to Social Ministry*, Phil Cooke's media-and-ministry titles, the Pro Church Tools ecosystem): these share the theology-of-platform argument but rarely descend to CTR, retention graphs, and keyword tools.
- **Creator-economy manuals** (Sean Cannell and Benji Travis's *YouTube Secrets* and its imitators): these have the tactics — thumbnails, watch time, vidIQ, TubeBuddy — but no ministry frame and no pastoral trust.

DiDio's differentiation is that he imports the second category's tactics wholesale into the first category's mission, with a charismatic-church accent no competitor has. The "Prophetic Word" keyword case study — identifying a high-volume, low-competition search phrase and building a content cluster around it — is simultaneously his best growth story and his clearest niche marker.

### Why "Occupy the Algorithm" beats "YouTube for Churches"

This is the most instructive marketing decision in the whole project, and it is worth dwelling on because the book itself explains it without meaning to.

*YouTube for Churches* is a search phrase. It is, ironically, exactly the kind of title Chapter 2 tells churches to stop using: accurate, generic, and value-free — the book-title equivalent of "Sunday Morning 9-8-2024," a title DiDio mocks as functionally writing "DO NOT WATCH" on your own thumbnail. It names the category but makes no promise, provokes no curiosity, and dates itself to a single platform.

*Occupy the Algorithm* does four things the working title cannot:

1. **It carries a conviction, not a category.** "Occupy" is a biblical imperative (Luke 19:13, "Occupy till I come" — a KJV resonance Drew will appreciate) with connotations of stewardship and territorial claim. The title turns a how-to manual into a mandate.
2. **It names the antagonist and claims it as mission field.** "The algorithm" is what church leaders fear and resent; the title says *take it*. That is the book's actual thesis — the algorithm is not the enemy, it is Roman roads.
3. **It follows the book's own doctrine.** Chapter 2's rule is that a title "should make a promise" and provoke curiosity rather than merely describe. The publisher applied the author's teaching to the author's own package.
4. **It is platform-proof and quotable.** "YouTube" in a title starts expiring the day the platform pivots; "the algorithm" survives every platform shift the book itself predicts ("what works today may not work tomorrow" is a recurring caveat).

The lesson: the *working* title was written for search; the *published* title was written for conviction. Trade publishing chose conviction — because books, unlike sermons on YouTube, are sold by felt need, not by query matching.

## 2. The Promise: What Page One Commits To, and How the Book Delivers

Page one makes a compound promise through the witch story and its immediate aftermath: **your existing content can reach the genuinely lost — not just your congregation — and this book will show you how, cheaply, in the margins of your week.** Chapter 1 closes the frame with a line that functions as the book's thesis statement: "What a shame it would be if we didn't evangelize the world at a time when it is easiest to do so."

The delivery is genuinely systematic, which is what separates this from most ministry-adjacent inspiration books:

- **Named, repeatable frameworks.** "Evan's Thumbnail Laws," the "ASQ (Answer Specific Questions) Strategy," "The Single Video Strategy," "Chop the Chicken" (his term for cutting a service into short clips). Naming a method once lets him invoke it later without re-teaching it — a structural discipline worth noting for reasons that will matter in Section 6.
- **A seven-step "viewer journey"** (eyes → facial expression → thumbnail text → title → timestamp → description → first seconds) that converts vague advice about "engagement" into an ordered checklist a volunteer can audit against.
- **Case studies with numbers.** The Kim Clement video that stalled at roughly 1,000 views in 24 hours, then passed 460,000 after a title-and-thumbnail change with no change to the content. The mega-ministry whose 90-second countdown was watched by only ~3% of viewers while losing more than 60% of them. The ministry that stopped buying promoted views, watched daily views fall from 40,000 to 10,000, then rebuilt to higher organic numbers with better engagement. Consolidating three streaming platforms into one and doubling live viewership. Every major claim is anchored to an observed outcome.
- **Honest cost accounting.** He tells readers to expect 50–100 well-executed videos before meaningful results, and jokes that "Overnight success gets soggy faster than overnight oats." Refusing to promise quick wins is itself a promise-keeping device: it tells the reader the author is describing a real practice, not selling a course.

Where delivery falls short of the page-one promise is at the spine of the story: the manuscript asserts salvations, deliverances, and healings resulting from the channel but never dramatizes a single one past the opening witch anecdote. The book's own audit notes flag this — recommending a real viewer-conversion testimonial be inserted on page 2 — which confirms a publisher saw the same gap: the evangelistic promise is proven statistically but not narratively.

## 3. Platform and Authority: How Credibility Is Built In-Text

DiDio never presents a résumé, a degree, or a bio paragraph. Authority is established entirely through four in-text mechanisms, and the mix is instructive:

1. **Practitioner receipts.** He runs the channel he describes (Encounter Today), and the numbers he cites are his own: the 460,000-view video, the 28.2% senior-viewer statistic, the consolidation experiment. The manuscript is studded with "SCREENSHOT" placeholders for his actual analytics — a receipts-first culture where the dashboard, not the author, makes the claim.
2. **Consultant war stories.** Twice he opens a chapter sitting across from a "mega-ministry" that asked him for help — the countdown autopsy in Chapter 6, the bought-views intervention in Chapter 3. The implicit message: ministries larger than yours already pay for this advice.
3. **Spiritual lineage.** The Sumrall passage does double duty. It argues the thesis (use the tools of the age), but it also positions DiDio inside a charismatic apostolic succession — Sumrall, Wigglesworth, Howard Carter, Azusa Street (he even sells "Wigglesworth Blend" ministry coffee). For his tribe, this is denominational credentialing more persuasive than any degree.
4. **The ownership posture.** The most authority-building sentence in the book is not a boast but a confession: "I choose to believe the performance is my fault if it underperforms." A man who takes blame for a video's failure is presumed competent to fix yours. He explicitly calls this "extreme ownership," and it runs through every case study: the sermon was never the problem; the packaging — his job — was.

Note what is absent: no false modesty, no borrowed authority from endorsements inside the text, no appeals to platform size for its own sake. The credibility is *demonstrated method* plus *demonstrated fruit*, which is the only kind of authority a first-time author can actually manufacture on the page.

## 4. Voice: What Makes It Distinct and Quotable

The voice is a fusion that competitors in neither adjacent category can replicate: **revivalist urgency wearing an analyst's toolbelt.** Its marketable components, each observable in the text:

- **Aphoristic compression.** The book's best lines are single sentences that could survive on a social graphic: "If loving people is your priority, then social media is your priority because that's where they are." Or, from the keyword chapter: "'SEO' is really about souls, not algorithms." These are the lines a marketing team builds a campaign from, and the manuscript produces one or two per chapter.
- **Blunt second-person verdicts.** Chapter 2 opens "Let me be blunt" and means it; generic titles get "Seriously, who wants to watch that?" He interrupts the reader mid-paragraph with "Read that again" and ends Chapter 1 asking the reader directly what *they* see when they look at the field. The reader is never an observer; the reader is being confronted.
- **Sanctified provocation.** The witch in the livestream. Sumrall's hypothetical "Irish right-hook" in Heaven. A section titled "The 'CCLI' Lie." He deliberately runs close to the edge of what a church book is allowed to say, and that edge is the brand.
- **The spiritual-tactical metaphor engine.** Paul's Roman roads become digital highways; every video is "a seed"; duplicate streams are water split into two weak channels; a keyword is a clear label on a medicine bottle. He repeatedly converts platform mechanics into biblical or pastoral imagery — which is precisely what makes the technical material feel like ministry rather than marketing.
- **The 2 AM searcher.** His most emotionally perceptive move is recurring: behind every search query is a desperate person — the insomniac typing "how to save my marriage" at 2 AM. It is the passage where tactics and compassion fully merge, and it is the book's moral center.

A publisher hears in this voice a author who can carry a podcast tour, a conference stage, and a short-form clip strategy — the voice *is* the platform extension.

## 5. Weaknesses a Publisher Would Flag

The manuscript's candor (it preserves its own audit layers) makes these easy to document:

1. **Visible scaffolding shipped as prose.** Outline headings pasted directly into the text ("- Value-Driven Content: The importance of providing valuable content to engage viewers" as a section head), a one-line stub where the funnel illustration should be, "SCREENSHOT" placeholders throughout, and a Reference Section consisting of a single sentence. The published book clearly required — and the included audits show it received — heavy developmental work.
2. **Unsourced and inconsistent statistics.** "According to one study" and "Medium.com found" carry the book's most important claims; the online-viewers-before-visiting figure appears as 33% in Chapter 1 and ~35% in Chapter 5; 28.2% is called "the majority" of views. The author notes themselves flag "Statistics Verification Needed." In a trade book, unsourced numbers are a liability both legally and reputationally.
3. **Structural repetition.** Chapters 8 (SEO) and 9 (keyword research) substantially overlap: search volume vs. competition is explained twice, title/description/tag implementation is taught twice, and "YouTube is the second largest search engine" appears in both. Value-driven titling is taught in Chapters 1, 2, and again in 9. This is the same disease *Reasoning Faith* struggles with — recurring material re-taught instead of referenced — and it is the first thing a developmental editor would consolidate.
4. **Shelf-life exposure.** Named tools (Opus Clip, vidIQ, TubeBuddy, Teespring), monetization thresholds, and platform mechanics date fast — the author twice concedes his own advice may be obsolete by the time of reading, and his "Tags are Dead?" section documents a tactic expiring inside his own manuscript. The retitle to *Occupy the Algorithm* partially hedges this; the interior content cannot fully escape it.
5. **Denominational dialect narrows the market.** Deliverance ministry, prophetic words, Kim Clement, "generals," the anointing — the book's proof-of-concept case studies live inside charismatic vocabulary. The first audit explicitly recommends explaining "deliverance" for outsiders. A Baptist or mainline media director can use every tactic but may not survive Chapter 1's framing.
6. **Assertion outrunning evidence at key points.** The bought-views collapse is one case presented as a law; the Discord etymology offered ("Discussion in one cord") is folk etymology and simply wrong; "the wealth of the wicked is laid up for the just" as a monetization frame would draw theological review at most houses. Small errors in checkable facts tax the credibility of the uncheckable claims.
7. **The missing conversion narrative,** discussed in Section 2 — the book's emotional thesis (souls, not views) is never given a fully told story beyond the opening anecdote.

None of these are fatal; all of them are the ordinary distance between a practitioner's manuscript and a publishable book. What made this book acquirable despite them is the subject of the final section.

## 6. The Acquisition Lesson for *Reasoning Faith*

Apologetics is a more crowded shelf than church-media, and it is dominated by established names. An editor scanning that shelf says yes for the same four reasons an editor said yes to a volunteer media director from North Carolina — and each has a direct application to Drew's manuscript. These are calibrated to what *Reasoning Faith* has committed to being: educational, informative, and self-convicting, with the reader examined through the Word. Nothing below imports DiDio's humor; everything below imports his *mechanics*.

### 6.1 Hook: open with a scene that embodies the thesis, not a statement of it

DiDio's book does not open with an argument for digital evangelism. It opens with a witch in a Wednesday-night livestream — one concrete human moment that *contains* the entire book (the camera reaches people the sanctuary never will), followed immediately by the indictment ("the #1 mistake churches make") and the stakes. Argument arrives after the reader is already implicated.

*Reasoning Faith*'s formal register does not prevent this; it changes the kind of scene. Not comedy — **witness**. Open the book, and ideally each chapter, with a specific, real moment where reasoning met faith and one of them buckled: a believer who could not answer their own child; a conversation where a Christian discovered the argument they were repeating was one they had never examined; a night the author's own certainty was interrogated. This is the direct remedy for the manuscript's personal-connection weakness: personality enters through *story placement*, not through jokes or register change. DiDio's warmth is almost entirely a function of where his anecdotes sit — first, before the teaching — and *Reasoning Faith* can adopt that placement while keeping every sentence formal.

### 6.2 Felt need: name the reader's ache before teaching anything — and make the ache self-facing

DiDio never sells "learn YouTube"; he sells "your sermon dies on Sunday" and lets the tactics follow the wound. In a crowded apologetics market, "learn the arguments" is not an acquirable felt need — a dozen better-platformed authors already own it. But *Reasoning Faith* has a felt need almost no competitor is positioned to claim, and it is the book's stated conviction: **the unexamined faith of the believer, not the unbelief of the skeptic.** Most apologetics arms the reader against other people. A book whose interrogation turns inward — where the reader is made to test whether they believe what they defend, Scripture functioning as the examining instrument — occupies nearly empty shelf space. That is a positioning sentence an editor can repeat in a pitch meeting, which is the practical definition of acquirability. Page one should perform this the way DiDio performs his: present the wound (a faith that has never been cross-examined), present the stakes, and only then present the method.

### 6.3 Authority: receipts, not résumé

DiDio's credibility is built from demonstrated method (the 1,000-to-460,000 repackaging), demonstrated ownership ("the performance is my fault"), and demonstrated fruit — never from a bio. *Reasoning Faith*'s equivalents are already within reach of its existing apparatus: worked examples where the method visibly operates on the page — a KJV word study that overturns a lazy reading in front of the reader; an objection dismantled step by observable step rather than reported as dismantled; real questions from real people answered in full. The authority move for Drew is *show the reasoning working*, at full length, at least once per chapter, the way DiDio shows a retention graph. And DiDio's ownership posture translates directly into the self-convicting register: an author who examines himself on the page first — who takes the book's medicine in view of the reader — earns the standing to examine the reader. That is the formal-register equivalent of "the performance is my fault."

### 6.4 Audience clarity: know whose 2 AM search this book answers

DiDio writes to one person and answers that person's objections in the order they would raise them. His deepest line — behind every search term is a person, desperate at 2 AM — is an audience-selection principle disguised as sentiment. *Reasoning Faith* must make the same choice explicitly: the doubting believer? the parent facing a child's questions? the churchgoer whose faith is inherited and untested? Each is a different book with different chapter openers. An editor's yes requires that the answer be visible on page one and stable through the last chapter. Drew's version of the 2 AM searcher is worth writing down and pinning above the manuscript: *behind every objection is a soul — and the first soul examined is the reader's own.*

### 6.5 Two structural disciplines to borrow outright

- **Name the frameworks.** DiDio's "Thumbnail Laws," "ASQ," and "Single Video Strategy" let him teach a concept once and *reference* it forever after. *Reasoning Faith*'s repetition problem is substantially a naming problem: recurring moves that are re-explained because they were never christened. Name the book's method and its recurring devices (the Reasoning Reflection already exists as one such asset), then invoke the name where the manuscript currently re-teaches. This is the single cheapest fix for the repetition weakness — and note that DiDio's own manuscript, which *failed* to do this across Chapters 8–9, shows exactly what the disease looks like when untreated.
- **Refuse false comfort.** DiDio's 50-to-100-videos honesty builds more trust than any promise. *Reasoning Faith*'s counterpart: say plainly that self-examination is slow, uncomfortable, and unfinished in one reading. In a self-convicting book, honesty about cost is not a marketing risk; it is the proof the author means it.

### 6.6 The title test

The retitle from *YouTube for Churches* to *Occupy the Algorithm* — category label to conviction — is the compressed version of every lesson above. *Reasoning Faith* passes the first half of that test: it is a charge, not a category, and its two words carry the book's method. What the DiDio case adds is the second half: the **subtitle and page one must carry the felt need and the promise**, because conviction titles sell only when the ache they answer is named within seconds of the reader picking the book up. That pairing — convicting title, need-naming subtitle, scene-first opening — is what made an editor say yes to a church media manual, and it is the shortest path to making an editor say yes in a far more crowded market.

---

*One final observation from the manuscript's unusual transparency: the document preserves two full editorial audit passes and the author's revision notes. The book an editor acquired was not the book the author first wrote — it was a practitioner's raw system plus a disciplined development process. That process, not polish at submission, is what converted conviction into a publishable book, and it is the working model this analysis recommends for* Reasoning Faith.
