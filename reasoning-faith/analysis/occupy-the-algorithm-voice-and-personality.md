# How Evan's Personality Lives on the Page
## A developmental analysis of *Occupy the Algorithm* (working title *YouTube for Churches*) by Evan DiDio — prepared for Drew, author of *Reasoning Faith*

---

### Scope note

This analysis is based on the full Google Docs manuscript (ten chapters plus front matter and a reference stub, ~239,000 characters received, not truncated). The document also contains two appended editorial layers — a chapter-by-chapter improvement audit and a line-edited rewrite of several chapters — plus a cleaned Chapter 1 with author notes. Because those layers partly smooth and extend the author's voice, **every observation below is anchored to the original ten chapters**, where Evan's prose is unmediated. The manuscript contains placeholder markers (e.g., "SCREENSHOT") where images will go; these do not affect the prose analysis. A web search for the published edition returned no indexed results, so publication context could not be verified; the analysis stands on the manuscript itself.

One framing remark before the sections: this is not a well-polished book at the sentence level. There are typos, inconsistent formatting, and structural looseness. That matters for you, Drew, because the book's warmth demonstrably does **not** come from polish. It comes from a set of repeatable authorial behaviors — and behaviors transfer across registers in a way that polish never needs to.

---

## 1. The Authorial Persona: Who the Reader Is Sitting With

The reader of this book is sitting with a **working media director who has made every mistake he warns against, kept the receipts, and holds himself — not the reader — responsible**. Three construction choices produce that impression, and all three operate at the sentence level.

**First: the persona is built from liability, not credentials.** The book contains no bio paragraph, no list of achievements, no "in my years of experience." Instead, Evan introduces himself through weakness and subordination. In Chapter 1 he writes, "To be honest I'm not the best marketer. Marketing feels weird to me, and that's ok." His authority position in his own church is framed as service to someone above him — his pastor preaches, and Evan's whole job is described as packaging another man's sermon for a platform. In Chapter 10 he confesses a present-tense shortcoming: "Even at the time of writing this book I personally feel the need to increase this responsibility" (regarding appreciating volunteers). The reader trusts him precisely because he never claims to have arrived. Authority is inferred from results (Section 4); it is never asserted.

**Second: the persona is constructed through interruption.** Evan's paragraphs behave like a person talking, because he constantly breaks his own flow to address the reader: "(Read that again)"; "Let me explain…"; "(Correct me if I'm wrong)"; "Don't get me wrong." That last pair matters most. "Correct me if I'm wrong" — dropped mid-analytics-claim in Chapter 4 — is a teacher inviting the student to check his work. It converts a monologue into a live conversation where the reader has standing.

**Third: the persona has a fixed moral center that every technical topic must pass through.** Thumbnails, analytics, SEO, monetization — each is re-grounded, within a sentence or two, in souls. The clearest instance closes Chapter 1's value section: "We've seen people saved, delivered, set free from addictions, healed, and eternally changed because we spent 15 minutes on a thumbnail/title." The sentence yokes the most mundane task in the book (a thumbnail) to the highest stake in his theology (eternity). Because that yoking happens in *every* chapter — monetization is "funding the work God has called us to do," keyword research is "understanding how your audience communicates" so the message reaches them — the reader never feels marketed to. They feel pastored by someone who happens to know software.

The humor (the Sumrall "Irish right-hook" joke, "overnight oats," "Chop the Chicken") sits **on top of** this structure, not inside it. Strip every joke from Chapter 1 and the persona survives intact: confessing, interrupting, morally anchored. That is the single most important finding for your project.

## 2. First-Person Testimony Mechanics: How the War Stories Work

The book deploys roughly one anchor story per chapter. The pattern is remarkably consistent:

| Chapter | Story | Approx. length | Placement | Who fails in it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A witch joins the Wednesday-night livestream | ~90 words | **First paragraph of the book** | Nobody — it establishes stakes |
| 2 | Kim Clement video flops, is retitled, hits 460,000 views | ~150 words | End of chapter, as case study | **Evan** (bad packaging) |
| 3 | Large ministry buying views; views drop 75% when they stop, then recover higher | ~180 words | Mid-chapter | The ministry (Evan diagnoses) |
| 4 | Streaming to three platforms with 25 viewers each; consolidating doubles viewers | ~80 words | Mid-chapter | **Evan's church** |
| 4 | "Older congregants can't use YouTube" — taught seniors one Wednesday; most already used it | ~60 words | Late chapter | **Evan's church** ("we believed it too") |
| 6 | Mega-ministry's 90-second countdown: 3% watched it, 60%+ of viewers lost | ~200 words | **Opens the chapter** | The ministry (Evan diagnoses) |
| 9 | "Prophetic Word" keyword discovery drives channel growth | ~100 words | Early, as "real-world example" | Nobody — a win, told with numbers |
| 10 | The whole chapter is the church's team model: 12 positions, 3-weeks-on-1-off rotation | full chapter | — | — |

Five mechanics are worth extracting:

**Stories are short.** The longest sustained narrative in the book is about 200 words. Instruction dominates the word count at roughly 4:1 or 5:1. The story never becomes memoir; it earns the chapter's thesis in flesh, then hands off to teaching immediately. The witch story is a masterclass in compression: incident, uncertainty ("I'm not sure where she is today"), theological frame ("it sowed a seed that someone else will water"), and pivot to thesis — all in one paragraph.

**Stories lead with failure or skepticism, and the failure is usually his.** The Kim Clement case study opens "This video FLOPPED in the first couple of days. Let me explain…" — the flop is announced *before* the triumph. The Facebook-streaming lie is introduced with the book's most disarming sentence: "I know this is a lie because we believed it too." When the failure belongs to a client ministry, Evan still keeps them anonymous and dignified ("a mega-ministry," "a large ministry") while making the numbers brutally specific. He names his heroes; he never names his negative examples.

**Stories carry the numbers; instruction carries the how.** Nearly every hard statistic in the book lives inside a narrative (1,000 views in 24 hours; 40,000 dropping to 10,000 per day; 25 viewers per platform; 3% watching the countdown). The teaching sections are comparatively number-light. This division of labor means the reader absorbs evidence as experience rather than as data.

**Placement follows a stakes-first logic.** When a chapter risks feeling dry (analytics, Chapter 6), the story opens it. When a chapter's teaching is intuitive (titles, Chapter 2), the story closes it as proof. Chapter 1 opens the entire book *in media res* — a witch in the chat — before a single statistic or principle appears. The reader is emotionally enrolled before they are instructed.

**Stories include an unresolved thread.** He doesn't know where the witch is today. The seed metaphor covers the uncertainty theologically, but the honesty of "I don't know how this ended" is itself a credibility device. Testimonies that all resolve neatly read as marketing; his don't.

## 3. Conviction and Directness: Why the Challenge Lands as Invitation

This book is startlingly confrontational for a niche how-to guide. It calls readers' current practice a "lie," says generic titles are "literally KILLING" channels, and titles its front matter "The Media Ministries' Mandate." Yet it never reads as scolding. Four mechanisms account for this — and all four are directly transferable to a self-convicting apologetics book.

**Mechanism 1: The author is convicted first.** Every accusation in the book passes through Evan before it reaches the reader. The extreme-ownership passage in Chapter 1 is grammatically first-person: "This requires me to realize it's my fault: to take extreme ownership." The book's boldest claim — bolded in the manuscript — is likewise self-directed: "I choose to believe the performance is my fault if it underperforms." When he exposes the Facebook-for-seniors lie, his church is the convicted party before the reader's is. The reader is never in the dock alone; the author is already standing there.

**Mechanism 2: Conviction is derived from the reader's own premises.** The book's most quotable line is a conditional syllogism: "If loving people is your priority, then social media is your priority because that's where they are." Chapter 3 repeats the form: if you truly believe the message you preach, you should be equally committed to getting it out. Note what this does: the *reader supplies the major premise* (of course I love people; of course I believe the message), and the conviction follows by logic, not by the author's opinion. The reader convicts themselves. Evan never has to raise his voice, because the reader's own confession does the pressing. This is the precise engine a self-convicting book needs.

**Mechanism 3: The challenge is posed as a question aimed at the reader's perception, then left open.** Chapter 1 ends its harvest passage: "When I see the field, I see a generation ripe for harvest - what do you see?" Chapter 5 does it again — someone joins your stream: what do they see? The interrogative form matters. A declarative ("you are ignoring the harvest") lectures; the question hands the reader the verdict and requires them to render it against themselves. He does not answer his own question. The silence after it is where the conviction happens.

**Mechanism 4: Conviction is immediately followed by companionship and a fix.** After diagnosing that most churches post content only insiders care about, he adds — in the same breath — that the reader shouldn't worry if that's them, "because we're about to fix it." Chapters end in first-person-plural enlistment: "so let's get to work"; "Let's fix that." The mandate language ("Jesus' orders for us remain unchanged") is always *us*, never *you*: the command binds author and reader identically. Shame is never left standing without a door out of it, and the author walks through the door with the reader.

One more note on the mandate register: the book's most severe line — "What a shame it would be if we didn't evangelize the world at a time when it is easiest to do so" — is set apart typographically as a standalone display line. Severity is *formatted* as weight (isolation on the page) rather than as volume (exclamation, capitals). Where the manuscript does resort to caps ("KILLING," "YES!"), it is measurably weaker; the isolation device is the one worth keeping.

## 4. Concrete Specificity: Numbers, Names, and Dated Moments

The book's authority rests on a distinctive texture of specificity:

- **Falsifiable numbers, including unflattering ones.** 33% of attendees check a church's YouTube before visiting; 1,000 views in 24 hours vs. 200,000 after the retitle, now past 460,000; a client's views falling 75% (40,000/day to 10,000/day) for a month on his advice before recovering; 25 viewers per platform; 3% watching the countdown; it takes 50–100 videos done right before expecting results. The willingness to publish the *painful* numbers — the month of decline his own counsel caused, the "super encouraging" 50-to-100-video runway — is what makes the triumphant numbers believable.

- **Oddly precise decimals as honesty markers.** "In 2024 the majority of our views (28.2%) came from those 65+." A persuader would say "nearly a third." The uncharismatic precision of 28.2% signals that a real analytics dashboard, not a rhetorical need, produced the figure.

- **Named people, asymmetrically.** Heroes and sources are named: Dr. Lester Sumrall, Smith Wigglesworth, Howard Carter, Kim Clement, the ministry Encounter Today, even branded products ("Wigglesworth Blend" coffee). Failures are anonymized: "a mega-ministry," "a large ministry." The naming pattern is itself a character reference — the author honors publicly and corrects privately.

- **Dated, placed moments.** "That Wednesday night service." "One Wednesday night" when they taught the seniors YouTube. The negative example is a *dated title*: "Sunday Morning 9-8-2024." Time-stamping does double duty: it makes the story checkable and it makes the reader feel present at a specific occasion rather than reading a composite illustration.

- **Named tools and operational detail.** Opus Clip, Canva, vidIQ, TubeBuddy, Pushpay, Discord, a QR-coded Master Google Doc at each volunteer station, a three-weeks-on-one-off rotation, signed volunteer agreements with two-weeks' notice. This is the intimacy of the open workshop: the reader is shown the actual machinery of his church, not a genericized best practice. Chapter 10 is essentially his org chart published — a act of generosity that reads as trust.

The combined effect: **authority through auditability, intimacy through access.** He is believable because he could be checked; he is likeable because he hides nothing, including the coffee brand and the failure months.

## 5. Direct Address: "You," Objections, and the Isolated Line

**"You" is the book's default case.** The reader is addressed in the second person on nearly every page, and — critically — the "you" is *specific*: a church leader with a camera "likely sitting in the back of your sanctuary." The possessives do heavy lifting: your sanctuary, your pastor, your congregants, your channel. The reader's actual world is furnished inside the prose.

**Objections are voiced in the reader's own words, dignified, then reversed.** The canonical pattern appears in Chapter 1: "You might be thinking, 'But our church is small, can we really reach millions?'" — followed by an emphatic yes and the *reframing of the weakness as an advantage* (small means agile). Chapter 3 opens the same way: "I hear it all the time: 'We just don't have the time or resources...'" then, "And I get it," then the reversal ("But here's the reality—"). The three-beat structure — quote the objection, validate it, reverse it — is why the book never feels like a lecture: the reader's resistance is always given the floor before it is answered. Chapter 2 formalizes the device into Q&A blocks ("What if it feels like clickbait?"), anticipating the *moral* objections a church reader would feel, not just the practical ones.

**Aphorisms are staged as isolated display lines.** The manuscript repeatedly pulls one compressed sentence out of the paragraph flow and sets it alone as a sub-heading: "If loving people is your priority, then social media is your priority…"; "What a shame it would be…"; "The key to success is understanding the viewer"; "Consolidation is key"; "Many visitors will watch you online before they ever come in person." Two things to notice. First, each isolated line is the *distillation* of the argument around it — it is earned by its context, not decorative. Second, the isolation is the emphasis; the sentence is trusted to land without italics or exclamation. These lines are the book's memory system: a reader who forgets everything else leaves with five or six portable sentences.

**Imperatives are short and physical.** "Read that again." "Let's get to work." "Have some fun with it!" The commands are never longer than a breath. And the most interesting second-person move is *modeled interrogation*: Evan displays the question he asks himself — "What am I doing that directly correlates to value for the viewer?" — as a standalone line, and the reader inherits the discipline by watching him practice it. He doesn't command self-examination; he demonstrates it and lets the reader copy the habit.

## 6. Why the Book Feels Like a Person, Not a Manual

Pulling the threads together, five properties separate this book from the manual it could have been:

1. **A continuous "I" with liabilities.** The narrator persists across chapters, carrying the same weaknesses (not a marketer, still under-appreciating volunteers), the same heroes (Sumrall), the same employer (his pastor, Encounter Today). A manual has an implied author; this book has a *character* — and characters are constituted by their limitations, not their expertise.

2. **The prose keeps a listener in the room.** "Let me explain…", "Can you guess what happened?", "Correct me if I'm wrong," "Read that again" — the text is riddled with moves that only make sense if someone is listening and might respond. A manual addresses no one in particular; this book cannot grammatically exist without a reader present.

3. **Costs are named before promises.** Fifty to a hundred videos before results; a 75% traffic drop before recovery; "I know, super encouraging." Manuals promise outcomes; people who have actually done the thing tell you what it costs first. Truth-telling about the price is the book's deepest bid for trust.

4. **A visible, stable value hierarchy.** Souls over metrics, message over marketing, viewer over institution, faithfulness over virality — reasserted in every chapter, sometimes at direct cost to the chapter's own topic (he spends his monetization chapter demoting monetization: AdSense is "the last resort"). A manual optimizes for its subject; a person subordinates the subject to what they love more. Readers feel that subordination as integrity.

5. **Repetition of theme, rotation of vehicle.** The book says essentially one thing — package the message for the person you're trying to reach, and take responsibility when they don't receive it — perhaps thirty times. It never feels repetitive, because the *vehicle* rotates: story, statistic, aphorism, Q&A, syllogism, case study, org chart. This is directly relevant to the repetition problem in *Reasoning Faith*: repetition is not the enemy; unrotated repetition is. When a thesis must recur, change the vessel it arrives in, and let a story or a number carry it the second time instead of a restatement.

## 7. Transferable Moves for *Reasoning Faith*

Ground rules honored below: *Reasoning Faith* stays educational, informative, and self-convicting — the reader examines themselves through the Word. **No humor is imported.** Every move below is drawn from a humor-free mechanism observed above, and each is restated for a formal, KJV-anchored register. None requires loosening your diction.

**1. Open inside a moment, not a thesis.**
*Observed:* the book's first sentence is a witch joining a Wednesday-night stream — incident before principle.
*Transfer:* open each chapter (or at minimum Part-opening chapters) with one short, dated, placed paragraph — a conversation with a doubter, an evening the author's own reasoning failed him, a verse that arrested him on a particular day — then pivot to the doctrine. Formal precedent is abundant: Augustine's *Confessions* teaches theology through incident. Cap it near 150 words; the discipline of brevity is what keeps testimony from becoming memoir.

**2. Confess before you convict.**
*Observed:* "I know this is a lie because we believed it too"; every charge passes through the author first.
*Transfer:* before any passage that asks the reader to examine themselves against Scripture, show the author examined under the *same* verse and found wanting — specifically, not generically. Not "we all fall short," but the actual occasion the author's reasoning served his pride rather than his Lord. In a self-convicting book, this is load-bearing: the reader will only open themselves to the mirror they have watched the author stand in front of.

**3. Build conviction as a syllogism from the reader's own confession.**
*Observed:* "If loving people is your priority, then social media is your priority because that's where they are."
*Transfer:* this is the book's most powerful engine and the most natural fit for *Reasoning Faith*'s logical mode. Let the major premise be something the reader already professes ("If we confess the Scriptures are the very words of God…"), and let the convicting conclusion follow by inference ("…then our neglect of them is not busyness; it is unbelief"). The reader cannot dismiss the conclusion without surrendering their own confession. Conviction by entailment, not exhortation — Pascal's own method, and fully at home in a formal register.

**4. One anchor story per chapter; keep instruction dominant.**
*Observed:* one story of 75–250 words per chapter; roughly a 4:1 instruction-to-story ratio; the story carries the chapter's thesis in flesh and then yields.
*Transfer:* adopt the ratio as a rule. One testimony per chapter, placed either first (when the chapter's material is abstract and needs stakes) or as closing proof (when the argument is intuitive and needs evidence). This directly addresses the personal-connection deficit without changing the book's fundamentally educational character.

**5. Let stories lead with failure and keep one thread unresolved.**
*Observed:* "This video FLOPPED… Let me explain"; "I'm not sure where she is today."
*Transfer:* when recounting an apologetic encounter, resist the testimony-genre pull toward tidy endings. Lead with what the author got wrong; where an outcome is unknown, say so and let Providence hold it ("what became of him I cannot say; the seed was sown, and God giveth the increase"). Unresolved endings read as truth; resolved ones read as illustrations.

**6. End key arguments with a second-person question — and do not answer it.**
*Observed:* "When I see the field, I see a generation ripe for harvest - what do you see?"
*Transfer:* this is the purest self-convicting device in the source book, and it needs no informality. Close a chapter's central argument by turning its claim into a question the reader must answer against themselves — "What do you see?"; "And whom, reader, have you been sparing: the sinner, or yourself?" — then end the section. The white space after the question is where the Word does its work (Jas. 1:23–25 supplies the warrant: the man beholding himself in the glass). Discipline: at most one per chapter, or the device becomes a tic.

**7. Voice the objection in the reader's own words; grant it dignity; then reverse it.**
*Observed:* "You might be thinking, 'But our church is small…'" → "And I get it" → "But here's the reality—".
*Transfer:* the three-beat structure survives register translation cleanly: "Perhaps the reader objects that reason is the enemy of faith. The objection is not frivolous — many have wrecked their faith upon proud reasoning. Yet consider…" Two rules from the source: (a) state the objection *strongly enough that the objector would sign it* — Evan quotes real complaints verbatim, never straw men; (b) where possible, reverse the weakness into the strength (small church → agile church; honest doubt → the raw material of tested conviction). This one move, applied consistently, converts lecture into dialogue.

**8. Publish the unflattering numbers and the precise details.**
*Observed:* the 75% drop his own advice caused; 28.2%; 50–100 videos before fruit; named tools and dates.
*Transfer:* specificity is the antidote to the abstraction that makes formal prose feel remote. Give exact years, exact durations of the author's own seasons of doubt, exact counts of conversations, the actual verse and the actual objection — and include the costs: how long self-examination takes, how often the author's arguments failed to persuade, what obedience has actually cost him. Precision, especially unflattering precision, is how a formal book earns the trust an informal book buys with charm.

**9. State every mandate in the first person before extending it, and command in the plural.**
*Observed:* "It is my responsibility to format the packaging…"; "Jesus' orders for us remain unchanged"; "so let's get to work."
*Transfer:* mandate language scolds only when the author stands outside it. Render each duty first as the author's own ("It is my charge…"), then as *ours* — and note how naturally the KJV's own hortatory idiom serves here: "Let us therefore…" (Heb. 4:1, 4:11, 12:1) is first-person-plural enlistment already in your register. The reader should never receive a command the author is not visibly under.

**10. Distill each section into one isolated, load-bearing sentence.**
*Observed:* the standalone display lines ("If loving people is your priority…"; "What a shame it would be…") that function as the book's memory system.
*Transfer:* your Pascal-compression instinct maps directly onto this device. Rule set from the source: one per section, not stacked; earned by the argument around it (a distillation, never a decoration); emphasized by isolation on the page, not by italics or intensifiers. Bonus for the repetition problem: the aphorism *replaces* restatement — where you would repeat the point, set the compressed line instead and move on.

**11. Model the self-interrogation you want the reader to perform.**
*Observed:* Evan displays his own recurring question — what am I doing that directly serves the viewer? — as a standalone line, and the reader inherits the habit.
*Transfer:* rather than instructing the reader to examine themselves, *show the author's own standing questions* — the questions he puts to himself before he opens his mouth to a skeptic, the verse he reads against his own motives each morning. Displayed once, formatted apart, first person. The reader copies practices they watch; they resist practices they are assigned.

**12. Re-ground every technique in the highest stake — within two sentences.**
*Observed:* thumbnail → "people saved, delivered… eternally changed"; keyword research → understanding how the lost speak so the message can reach them.
*Transfer:* in an apologetics book the equivalent risk is arguments admired as arguments — reasoning as trophy rather than as rescue. After each apologetic method or word study, return within a sentence or two to the soul it exists to serve and the God it exists to honor. This recurring re-grounding is what made a marketing manual feel like ministry; it is what will keep a reasoning manual feeling like discipleship.

### What not to import — and what to keep from those same passages

Leave behind: the jokes and whimsy (the "Irish right-hook," "overnight oats," "Chop the Chicken"), the snark ("Seriously, who wants to watch that?"), ALL-CAPS emphasis, exclamation stacking, social-media asides ("DM me on Instagram"), and the casual punctuation. But note carefully what remains when those are removed: the witch story contains no joke; the mega-ministry countdown story contains no joke; "I know this is a lie because we believed it too" contains no joke. The warmth of this book is not located in its humor. It is located in confession, precision, voiced objection, derived conviction, and the open question — and every one of those travels into a formal register without loss. The humor is Evan's accent. The moves above are his voice. Take the voice.
