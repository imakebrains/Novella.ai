# Story Inventory — Protocol and Veiling Ethics

Every report in this packet says some version of "insert your stories." None
of them can say *which* stories, because those live in the author's memory.
This protocol turns that gap into a working document.

**Where the filled inventory lives:** in Google Drive with the manuscript,
NOT in this public repository. This file holds only the protocol, the
prompts, and the ethics rules. The filled inventory contains identifying
detail by design and is private raw material.

---

## 1. The harvest protocol

For each chapter (drafted and planned), collect candidates in four passes:

1. **The disguised ones.** Reread the chapter hunting for sentences that are
   "a memory in witness protection" — second-person or "a person" passages
   that are too specific to be invented. The emotional audit found several
   (Ch. 1's broken-person paragraph; Ch. 2's divided-prayer hypotheticals;
   Ch. 3's trusted-people-in-the-same-year line). Each one is a story
   already selected by instinct. List the real event behind it.
2. **The demand mirror.** Name the chapter's point of highest demand on the
   reader (examine your influences; unify your divided heart; submit to
   costly truth). Ask: when did I pay this price, or refuse to? That moment
   is the chapter's vulnerability beat.
3. **The Dedication ledger.** The Dedication compresses a whole season into
   a litany. Expand it privately into its component scenes — each line of it
   is a chapter-grade story. Assign each scene to the chapter whose argument
   it embodies.
4. **The ordinary anchors.** For the totemic-object motif: which physical
   objects, places, and times of day carry the season's memory? (The comp
   analysis shows objects out-perform abstractions at carrying feeling.)
   These become cold-open material and callbacks.

## 2. Veiling and compositing ethics

The interview settled the disclosure posture: vignettes, some veiled or
composited, no obligation to expose anything raw. These rules keep that
posture honest — the genre research is blunt that a persona caught posing
(the Strobel backlash) loses exactly the readers this book wants most.

1. **Never invent an event and present it as testimony.** Fabricated
   "true stories" are the genre's unforgivable sin. A hypothetical is fine
   when framed as one ("Imagine a man who…"); it must never wear the "I."
2. **Veil people, not feelings.** Change names, places, timing, and
   circumstantial detail freely to protect real people. Do not soften what
   it felt like or what it cost — that is the part the reader needs true.
3. **Composite with disclosure.** Combining details of separate real events
   into one narrative is legitimate with a standard author's note (front
   matter): "Some stories in this book have had details altered or combined
   to protect the privacy of others. The events and what they cost are
   true." One sentence, once, covers the whole book.
4. **Other people get mercy on the page.** Anyone identifiable who is not
   the author appears only with consent or beyond recognition — especially
   anyone who wounded the author. The book's own rule (conviction without
   contempt) applies doubly to its stories: the author may bleed on the
   page; nobody else may be bled by it.
5. **Scars, not wounds.** Nothing goes on the page that is still open. If a
   story cannot yet be narrated with perspective, mark it off-limits and
   write the chapter's beat from an older scar. The reader can tell the
   difference between a gift and a plea.
6. **Purpose test.** Every disclosure must serve the chapter's idea and the
   reader's transformation. A story whose only function is to be about the
   author gets cut, however good it is.
7. **The Dedication's people stay veiled.** The season it describes involves
   another person. On the page, that person remains unidentifiable —
   the story is told as what happened *in the author*, not what someone
   else did. The wound is the author's material; the other person's story
   is not.
